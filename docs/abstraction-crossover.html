NashForge · heads-up no-limit Hold'em · counterfactual regret minimization

The Abstraction Crossover

A finer card abstraction costs 5.4× more per iteration and is blind to draws. Below roughly ten minutes of training it loses to the cheap one. Above it, it wins — on a fifth of the opponent's iterations.

01 Withdrawn

Everything published before August was measuring the wrong thing

This project previously trained agents with an evolutionary algorithm and published scaling laws, tournament rankings and hyperparameter findings. An audit conducted by executing the code — not reading it — found that the fitness function scored the wrong player, and that the deck was re-seeded with the same seed every hand, so agents were evaluated on two repeated hands.

+451 BB/100 → −4.8 ± 29.6

An untrained random network's score under the original fitness function, and under the corrected one. Approximately zero is what a random agent among random agents must score. Reported bests ran to 5213 BB/100, where 50 is a crushing professional win rate.

The sanity check was always available and never run: poker is zero-sum, so in self-play the population mean must be about zero. It was large and positive in every logged run. Every result derived from that metric has been withdrawn, and the 13,816 lines of pipeline that produced them were removed from the working tree — recoverable by name from the pre-cfr-pipeline tag rather than by archaeology.

The rules engine survived intact. Chip conservation across 2,400 randomised hands, side pot construction and allocation, the wheel straight, short all-ins that correctly do not reopen betting: all verified against known-correct answers. That is the part of a poker system that is hard to write, and it works. What failed was thin, recent, and cheap to replace.

02 Validated

What replaced it, and why it can be believed

Counterfactual regret minimization converges toward a Nash equilibrium in imperfect-information games, and — more to the point here — it comes with measures that say how far from one you are. CFR does not play a game, it traverses one, which the existing engine could not support: it mutates state in place and consumes a dealt deck. The betting was rebuilt as small immutable state carried in the node.

A solver that has converged to the wrong thing looks exactly like one that has converged, so three independent checks pin it down. Kuhn poker has an analytically known game value of −1/18 to the first player, and the solver reproduces it. Leduc Hold'em is small enough to traverse exactly, so its exploitability is computed rather than estimated — and must fall toward zero.

No-limit cannot be traversed at all — the opening deal alone has about 1.6 million outcomes — so the abstract game has to fit in memory instead. That is arithmetic rather than guesswork: the betting tree is finite and small, so it is enumerated, and the total is that count multiplied by the buckets available per street.

The raise cap, not the bucket count, is what decides feasibility: a second raise per street multiplies the game by roughly 150×. Every experiment below therefore uses one raise per street and six buckets — a choice that turns out to matter in section 05.

03 Measured

Two ways to describe a hand, and what each costs

A card abstraction groups hands the solver may treat identically. Postflop, two signals were built and both measured, because which to use is a genuine trade-off rather than an optimisation.

Monte-Carlo equity estimates the chance of winning against a random hand. It accounts for draws, and costs 44.05 ms per solver iteration. Made-hand strength scores only the hand made so far, in a single deterministic evaluation, at 8.11 ms — 5.4× cheaper, free of sampling noise, and provably blind to draws: on a two-heart board, the nut flush draw and the same two overcards without it land in the same bucket.

So the cheap signal buys about five times more iterations with a worse map. Three separate measurements failed to find the blindness costing anything, which is the question section 04 exists to settle.

04 Measured

The crossover

The practical question is "given N seconds of training, which bucketing should I use?" — and it does not require exploitability at all. Train one agent per signal to the same wall-clock budget, play them against each other, and count chips. Each agent is asked the question its own bucketing poses, so neither is handed the other's key and made to look like a bad player rather than a mismatched lookup.

The equity agent wins with 60,117 iterations against made-hand's 288,050 — a fifth of the training, and it still wins, at about 7.8σ. All three seeds agree in direction at every budget, and all three separate individually at the top rung (+1.152, +0.786, +0.811).

The blindness to draws does cost something. It just takes about ten minutes of training before the better map is worth the five times longer it takes to read.

What the figure does not show

The crossing point is somewhere between 640s and 2560s and these budgets do not locate it more precisely. The 640s rung is where the seeds disagree (+0.053, +0.088, +0.678), which is why it pools to "not separated" rather than to a narrow result straddling zero.

The wide interval at 40s (±0.729, against per-seed errors near 0.35) is between-seed disagreement rather than measurement noise — the seeds give −3.026, −4.450 and −1.932. Short budgets produce genuinely different strategies from different seeds, so no single-seed figure should be quoted at that end of the scale.

Because the budget axis is wall-clock, this experiment measures the machine as much as the algorithm. Load average is sampled inside each agent's own training window and stored with every measurement. During this run one rung pair trained at load 4.0–4.5 and the rest near 1.0; the busy seed's values sit inside the spread of the quiet ones, and at the top rung the busiest seed gave the largest result. No systematic effect — which is not the same as proof of none.
05 Unresolved

The measurement that failed, and what it cost to find out

Beating the other abstraction is a relative claim. Both agents could be far from equilibrium and one merely less bad. The absolute claim needs exploitability, and in no-limit that means Local Best Response: a greedy exploiter that tracks what the opponent's betting reveals, then picks the action that looks best one step ahead. Because it plays only a subset of the strategies a true best response could, whatever it wins is a lower bound. A large value proves a strategy is exploitable. A small one proves nothing at all.

It never produced a usable bound on a converged strategy, and four defects and three valuation models were spent finding out why.

Four defects in the instrument

  • The opponent's range was carried across streets without re-bucketing. The abstraction fits a separate clustering per street, so bucket 3 on the flop and bucket 3 on the turn are unrelated categories; a read accumulated on one street was applied to different hands on the next.
  • Showdowns were always evaluated against the river's clustering, whatever street the belief was formed on — self-consistent only on the river.
  • The exploiter read its opponent's hole cards and excluded them from its own sampling pool, so its rollouts never considered the opponent holding the hand they actually held.
  • The comparison script named a winner from bounds that had gone slack, marking the lower value "ahead" unconditionally. At the deepest rung both values were negative, and it reported the abstraction the exploiter had lost more money to as the better one.

Fixing all four was worth +4.156 chips/hand and tightened the error bar by 22%. The bound still did not clear zero.

Three valuations, none of them right until the third

The remaining problem was how a raise is priced. Each version was a genuine correctness improvement, and each exposed the next layer.

The first version valued the showdown against the opponent's contribution before they responded, so every chip bet was pure downside and fold equity the only upside. The cheapest legal bet won by construction: the exploiter bet the smallest size offered in 47% of decisions, and kept betting it as the floor was lowered and its results got worse.

Counting the called chips fixed the underbetting and broke the other way. Equity was still evaluated against the opponent's whole range — but folding removes the weak hands, so whoever calls is stronger than average, increasingly so as the bet grows. The exploiter turned aggressive on 52% of decisions against a converged strategy and lost 4.9 chips a hand.

Both are approximations of the same correct treatment: condition on the response, then evaluate. A raise is now priced against the hands that would actually call it. Showdowns are sampled once per decision and re-weighted per candidate bet, so conditioning each bet on its own calling range costs almost nothing instead of a full set of rollouts each.

+2.783 → −2.900

The same strategy, untouched, read "proves exploitable" one afternoon and slack that evening. Only the exploiter's internal valuation changed. No exploitability figure this project produced before 15 August should be quoted — they are all sensitive to a modelling choice that was wrong until then.

Where it was stopped, and why

Four defects and three valuation models in, the exploiter still cannot beat a 59,050-iteration strategy at either raise cap. The investigation was stopped deliberately rather than abandoned. At some point the honest reading is that a greedy one-step exploiter genuinely cannot beat this strategy — which is a legitimate if weak result, not a defect still to be chased.

One earlier conclusion has to be withdrawn with it. Training at two raises per street did produce a bound clearing zero — but holding iterations fixed while the game grew 17.8× left that strategy at 0.132 iterations per information set, most of them visited less than once. It was not a converged strategy proving exploitable; it was an untrained one. This project's own data agrees: one raise per street at 4,075 iterations, density 0.177, measured +3.366 ± 1.001 — indistinguishable. Exploitability tracks training density, not raise cap.

Off-tree betting — letting the exploiter choose sizes the solver was never trained against, mapped back through the pseudo-harmonic translation of Ganzfried & Sandholm — is worth +2.5 chips/hand once the valuation is right, having been worth nothing under either earlier one. The machinery was correct; it was being driven badly.

06 Threats

What this does not establish

  • No exploitability claim is made. LBR bounds from below, and it never cleared zero on a converged strategy. Nothing here says either agent is near equilibrium.
  • The crossover is a relative result. Equity beats made-hand above ~640 seconds. Both may be weak in absolute terms; one is merely less so.
  • Action abstraction upper-bounds achievable exploitability regardless of how well the solver runs. A floor in the curve may be the six-action space rather than the algorithm, and that space was inherited from a policy network's output layer, not designed as a bet abstraction.
  • One abstraction family, one raise cap. Six buckets, k-means on a one-dimensional strength signal, one raise per street. Waugh et al. showed refining an abstraction can make the resulting strategy more exploitable, so granularity is something to measure rather than assume — and only two granularities were measured.
  • Wall-clock budgets measure the machine. Throughput and load are stored with every measurement precisely because they cannot be assumed constant, and one run was interrupted twice by system restarts.
  • The crossing point is not localised beyond "between 640s and 2560s", and the 640s rung is where the seeds disagree.
07 Open

What is still open

The second barrel. The exploiter's rollout assumes no betting after the modelled action — the standard LBR simplification and the last structural source of slack. It is also the most work, and nothing else is blocked on it.

Two raises per street at matched density. Answering the raise-cap question properly needs about 1.05M iterations rather than 59,050 — roughly 18× the cost — and is not worth spending until there is an exploiter whose numbers survive its own revision.

Localising the crossing point. Budgets between 640s and 2560s would narrow it; the current experiment establishes that it exists, not where.
